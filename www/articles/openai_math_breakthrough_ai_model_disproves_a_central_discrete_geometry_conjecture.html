AI Finds a New Route Through an 80‑Year‑Old Erdős Geometry Puzzle

OpenAI says one of its internal models has helped crack a problem Paul Erdős posed in 1946, by finding a construction that almost no one expected to work. The company claims this is the first time an AI system has autonomously produced a proof of a prominent open conjecture in pure mathematics, with human mathematicians then checking and refining the argument.

The problem lives in a deceptively simple setting: points on a flat plane and distances of length one. It has been a fixture of combinatorial geometry for nearly eight decades, and many experts had quietly decided that a certain Erdős‑style conjecture about it was probably true. That belief now appears to be wrong, at least for infinitely many values of (n).

What surprised people most was not just that the conjecture failed, but the route the AI took to get there: a detour through algebraic number theory—complete with class field towers and Golod–Shafarevich theory—tools usually reserved for deep questions about number fields, not point diagrams on graph paper.

A puzzle about points and unit distances

The “planar unit distance problem” asks a simple question. Put (n) points in the plane. How many pairs can you arrange to be exactly one unit apart?

If you lay points out on a square lattice—like intersections on a sheet of graph paper—you get lots of unit distances along horizontal and vertical lines, and sometimes along diagonals if you choose the spacing carefully. For decades, the best known constructions looked broadly like clever variations on such grid patterns.

Erdős conjectured that, no matter how smart you are in placing those points, the maximum number of unit‑distance pairs grows only slightly faster than linearly with (n), something on the order of (n^{1+o(1)}). Mathematicians tried to prove an upper bound of roughly that form, or to build examples that would beat it convincingly. Neither direction fully panned out: upper bounds lagged behind the conjectured behavior, and explicit constructions stubbornly refused to explode in the way a genuine counterexample would require.

Over time, a kind of working consensus formed that the spirit of the Erdős guess was probably right, and that square‑grid‑like patterns were, in a broad sense, close to optimal. The problem stayed open, but there was a strong sense that the answer should hug that near‑linear growth.

What the OpenAI model appears to have done

OpenAI did not train a special‑purpose theorem prover to attack this one question. According to TechCrunch’s reporting and the company’s own account, the team was testing a new, unreleased “general‑purpose reasoning model” on a range of Erdős‑style problems, probing how far current AI systems can go when asked to reason step‑by‑step.

Gary Marcus, summarizing OpenAI’s technical notes, describes the model as tuned for extended “chain‑of‑thought” reasoning: it effectively thinks out loud for long stretches, generating a detailed transcript of its attempts, partial ideas, and lemmas. Human mathematicians then sifted through that transcript looking for anything that might actually work.

In the case of the unit distance problem, the model produced an unexpected family of point configurations that clearly outperform the long‑assumed near‑linear barrier. OpenAI reports that, for infinitely many values of (n), the construction yields at least (n^{1+\delta}) unit‑distance pairs for some fixed positive constant (\delta). That is a qualitatively different statement from “barely above linear”: the exponent on (n) is strictly larger than 1 by a fixed amount, not one that fades toward zero as (n) grows.

Interesting Engineering notes that Princeton mathematician Will Sawin later refined the argument and showed that the improvement can be expressed with an explicit exponent, with (\delta) taken to be about 0.014. The exact value is small, but the existence of any fixed (\delta > 0) is what matters, because it contradicts the original Erdős‑inspired expectation.

Equally striking is how the construction is built. Instead of reasoning purely in terms of points and lines, the AI recast the problem using algebraic number theory. Reports from OpenAI and secondary coverage say the proof involves Gaussian integers, infinite class field towers, and Golod–Shafarevich theory—topics that typically appear in advanced number theory courses rather than in geometry seminars.

At a high level, the idea is to start with exotic number systems whose arithmetic has very rigid symmetries, exploit those symmetries to create many repeated “unit‑like” distances, and then embed that algebraic structure into the ordinary plane so that a large fraction of those distances become exactly length one. The repeated patterns in the number field show up as an unusually dense collection of unit distances between points.

I suspect most geometers never seriously tried this path; it sits outside the usual toolkit for discrete geometry, and humans only have so much patience for low‑probability bets. A model that happily explores dozens of arcane directions in parallel is, almost by design, going to behave differently.

How the proof was checked

OpenAI’s model did not output a polished, journal‑ready paper. It generated a long chain of reasoning, including dead ends, partial arguments, and candidate constructions. Professional mathematicians then had to figure out which parts were sound, and how to turn them into a conventional proof.

According to Marcus’s account and other write‑ups, human experts combed through the transcript, isolated the promising counterexample, and rebuilt the argument around it. Sawin carried out a detailed verification and sharpening of the proof, putting the core ideas into standard mathematical form and tightening the estimates to get an explicit exponent (\delta).

The version OpenAI has publicized, and that outside mathematicians say they have checked, now gives a rigorously stated theorem: for infinitely many (n), there exist planar point sets with strictly super‑linear many unit distances, beating the Erdős‑style conjectured upper behavior. The result still awaits the usual cycle of formal peer review, but several independent experts quoted in the coverage say they are satisfied that the main claim is correct.

This careful verification matters in part because OpenAI has been burned once already. As Slashdot notes, a former OpenAI vice president previously claimed on X that GPT‑5 had solved ten unsolved Erdős problems, only for it to emerge that the “solutions” were rediscoveries of known results from the literature. That episode has made both the company and outside observers more cautious about what counts as a genuine breakthrough.

Why mathematicians are paying attention

AI has been present in serious mathematics for years. Automated theorem provers, SAT solvers, and computer‑algebra systems have helped prove results that would be impractical by hand, and learning‑based tools have already assisted on some smaller Erdős‑style questions. So what makes this case feel different?

One reason is the status of the problem. The planar unit distance question is a classic: simple to state, central to discrete geometry, and widely known among specialists. It is not a contrived benchmark. Seeing an AI‑assisted proof upend the long‑standing conjectured behavior gives mathematicians a concrete example of a system contributing something they had not already half‑expected.

Another reason is the cross‑pollination. The model did not just grind through known geometric techniques faster; it reached into a relatively distant part of mathematics and pulled out heavy algebraic machinery that most geometers had never thought to try on this particular puzzle. Thomas Bloom, quoted by Marcus, argues that this kind of result plays to the strengths of large language models: they can combine “superhuman levels of patience” with familiarity with a wide range of technical tools, and are willing to keep pushing down lines of attack that a human might abandon as a waste of time.

The episode also illustrates a shift in how AI is being used. Instead of deploying a specialized, highly formal proof assistant, OpenAI leaned on a more free‑form reasoning model whose output looks a lot like a mathematician’s scratch‑work and email drafts. Human collaborators then performed the familiar mathematical labor of checking, simplifying, and extending the ideas.

From OpenAI’s point of view, mathematics serves as a stringent testbed for such systems. If a model can sustain a long, delicate argument that domain experts cannot easily break, the company argues, that same capacity might be valuable in other fields that require multi‑step reasoning, such as biology or physics. Whether that promise holds up outside pure math remains to be seen.

A milestone, but not the end of the story

Even if the result stands as stated, the unit distance problem is not fully “solved.” The new construction improves the known lower bound and shows that the widely believed Erdős‑style upper behavior cannot be correct for all (n), but the exact growth rate of the maximum number of unit distances remains unknown. Researchers are already asking whether similar number‑theoretic ideas can be brought to bear on related problems, such as Erdős’s distinct distances question.

There are also social and practical questions. How should credit be assigned between an AI model that proposes a construction, the company that trained it, and the human mathematicians who verify and refine the proof? What standards of transparency and reproducibility should apply when an opaque neural network plays a central role in discovery? Marcus, for one, has emphasized the need for robust verification procedures and clear communication about what the AI actually did versus what humans supplied.

On Hacker News, reactions to OpenAI’s announcement have been a mix of excitement and careful optimism. Some commenters see this as a sign that mathematicians today will be able to attack problems that once seemed out of reach; others note that the field is only just beginning to figure out how best to collaborate with these tools.

My sense is that this particular episode will be remembered less for the numerical value of (\delta) and more for the pattern it suggests. An AI system, treated as a general‑purpose reasoning partner, managed to connect an 80‑year‑old geometry puzzle to deep algebraic number theory and produce a construction human experts consider genuinely new. That does not replace mathematicians, but it does change the texture of the search space they work in.

Whether this marks the dawn of a “golden era” of AI‑guided mathematics, as some commentators have hinted, will only be clear in hindsight. For now, an old Erdős conjecture has been nudged aside, a fresh trail between number theory and combinatorial geometry has opened up, and a lot of human mathematicians are busy walking down it—sometimes with a patient, silicon collaborator at their elbow.
