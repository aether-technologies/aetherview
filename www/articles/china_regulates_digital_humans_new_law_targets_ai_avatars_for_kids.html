China's New Digital Human Regulations: Mapping the Future of AI Governance

China's Cyberspace Administration opened a regulatory door this week that signals how governments may approach the wild expansion of synthetic avatars and AI personas. On April 7, the agency released draft regulations targeting "digital humans"—a category that encompasses everything from realistic chatbot partners to virtual brand ambassadors. The proposal, open for public comment until May 6, represents perhaps the most comprehensive attempt yet to govern this emerging technology at scale. The timing matters. As artificial intelligence technologies become cheaper and more accessible, the potential for misuse has grown alongside legitimate applications. China's move comes as the country doubled down on aggressive AI adoption in its five-year policy blueprint released last month, creating an immediate tension: how to encourage innovation while preventing harm.

What Exactly Are Digital Humans?

Before parsing the regulatory framework, it helps to understand what authorities are actually trying to regulate. According to China's official definition, digital humans are "virtual figures that exist in nonphysical environments and simulate human appearance and behavior using technologies such as computer graphics, digital image processing and artificial intelligence". That's the technical definition. In practice, digital humans range from entertainment avatars in social media or gaming platforms to virtual customer service representatives, from AI-generated news anchors to romantic chatbot companions. The technology can replicate "voices, actions, interactivity and even personality traits of real people," whether based on actual individuals or entirely algorithmic creations. What makes digital humans particularly novel is their recent explosive growth. According to Chinese authorities, the sector has "seen rapid growth in recent years, injecting new momentum into sectors such as public services, entertainment and commercial marketing". This rapid expansion is exactly what prompted regulatory concern. As one expert noted, the technology's realism creates "significant risks of them being used to generate harmful content, spread rumors, incite crime or maliciously induce consumption".

The Regulatory Requirements

China's draft regulation establishes requirements across the entire supply chain. Service providers, distribution platforms, and users all bear responsibilities under what authorities call a "full-chain governance" approach. The most immediately visible requirement mandates that all digital human content be prominently labeled as such. This labeling must be clear and persistent—not buried in terms of service or hidden in metadata. The intent seems straightforward: users should know they're interacting with synthetic entities rather than humans. The regulation also introduces strict consent requirements. Before anyone's likeness, voice, or personal data can be used to create a digital human, explicit permission is required. This protects against deepfakes and unauthorized digital impersonations, addressing one of the most frequently cited harms associated with the technology. For minors, the restrictions become substantially more stringent. The draft explicitly bans "virtual intimate relationships" such as virtual family members or romantic partners for those under 18. It also prohibits digital human services designed to "induce excessive spending, encourage harmful behavior or negatively affect their mental and physical health"—language that clearly targets addictive-by-design features. Content restrictions round out the framework. Digital humans cannot disseminate material involving sexual innuendo, violence, horror, discrimination, or content endangering national security. Service providers are encouraged (though not mandated) to prevent sexually suggestive content, depictions of cruelty, or material inciting ethnic or regional discrimination.

Compliance Burdens for Tech Platforms

For gaming companies, social media platforms, and metaverse operators, these requirements translate into concrete operational changes. Most immediately, platforms need content moderation systems capable of identifying and labeling digital human content at scale. This isn't trivial—distinguishing between user-generated content featuring digital humans and other synthetic media requires either sophisticated AI classification or substantial human review. The consent verification system poses another challenge. Platforms must establish mechanisms to verify that creators possess explicit permission to use individuals' likeness or voices. For companies operating across jurisdictions, this means adding China-specific compliance layers to existing global infrastructure. The restrictions on services targeting minors create additional complexity. Many gaming and social platforms with youth audiences will need to audit or redesign digital human features to ensure they don't facilitate "virtual intimate relationships" or excessive spending patterns. This could mean removing certain chatbot companions, restricting access to digital avatars that mimic mentorship or familial relationships, or redesigning reward systems. For platforms operating in China, compliance will likely require dedicated resources. However, I suspect that many companies will interpret these requirements more broadly—the precedent of China's regulatory approach often influences global platform design, even outside China. We've seen this pattern with previous regulations on algorithmic recommendation systems and AI-generated content labeling.

The Broader Strategic Context

Understanding why China pursued these particular restrictions requires looking at the government's AI ambitions and concerns. The five-year plan explicitly prioritizes AI adoption across the economy, yet Chinese policymakers simultaneously worry about AI-enabled social disruption, misinformation, and challenges to state control. Digital humans occupy a peculiar position in this calculation. They're useful for government services and commercial applications, but they're also accessible to ordinary users—"with the lowering threshold of AI tools, ordinary users can now create and publish digital humans," as one academic noted. This democratization of synthetic media creation genuinely complicates governance. The emphasis on national security language in the draft—prohibiting digital humans from "endangering national security, inciting subversion of state power, promoting secession or undermining national unity"—reflects these concerns. It's not merely about consumer protection; it's about preventing synthetic personas from becoming vectors for political messaging outside state control.

Global Implications

China rarely regulates in isolation. Its approach to internet governance, data protection, and AI has historically influenced global norm-setting, sometimes through direct adoption by other nations, sometimes through the gravitational pull of a massive market that companies must accommodate anyway. Several aspects of the Chinese regulations preview likely future global trends. First, the "full-chain governance" concept—holding not just platforms but users and creators responsible—represents a departure from how many Western frameworks approach content regulation. This could gradually shift global norms toward greater liability for end users. Second, the emphasis on labeling synthetic media reflects a broader international movement. The EU's AI Act includes provisions on synthetic media transparency; other jurisdictions are considering similar requirements. China's implementation at scale could refine these approaches or expose practical challenges. Third, the minors-focused restrictions on addictive features and intimate relationships tap into genuine concerns that extend well beyond China. As digital avatars and AI companions proliferate, questions about age-appropriate interactions and psychological dependency will eventually reach regulators globally. China is essentially providing a regulatory template.

Looking Forward

The public comment period closing May 6 will likely see pushback from platforms operating in China and industry stakeholders advocating for clearer compliance pathways. The final regulation could differ meaningfully from this draft, though China's pattern suggests core requirements will persist. What seems clear is that digital humans have transitioned from pure technological novelty to a regulated category. Whether you see that as necessary guardrails against a genuinely risky technology or as overreach depends partly on your baseline assumptions about synthetic media. What's less debatable is that this represents a significant moment in AI governance—the point where the technology became common enough that governments felt compelled to establish formal rules rather than leaving it to industry self-regulation. For platforms, the immediate task is monitoring the regulation's finalization and beginning compliance planning. For technologists and researchers, it's an opportunity to study how real-world governance affects AI development trajectories. For everyone else, it's worth noting: the digital humans you'll encounter in apps, games, and services over the coming years will increasingly bear the marks of regulatory compromise between innovation and control.
